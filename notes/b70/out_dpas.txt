# E24 step 1 - integer XMX DPAS with byte-replicated operands: does the matrix path remove the
# per-row instruction slot?  (probe_dpas.cpp, notes/b70; measured 2026-10-29)

## What is being claimed and by what method

rev C costs `slots/weight ~= 5.8 + 1.15 * rows` (fitted from its own measured 205.5 -> 136.4
G-weights/s for rows 1 -> 4 against the 1.43e12 slot/s issue budget). The DPAS formulation under
test (b70-kernels.md 2c item 13b) replaces the byte-SUM with the four BYTES of
`window * 0x83DCD12D` as four K-steps, paired with the activation replicated 4x along K:

    sum_kb A[col][kb] * B[kb][t]  =  sum_k bytesum(prod_k) * x_k      exact, in int32

so nothing needs more than 8 bits (A is u8 as produced, B is s8), the accumulator is int32, and the
epilogue `k_inv * q * (acc - 510 * sum_x8)` is unchanged. Shape M=8, K=32, N=16, sub-groups of 16,
all proven reachable on this card by doc/b70/b70-bench (366.92 TOPS = 100% of Intel's 367).

STEP 1 DELIBERATELY MAKES OPERAND ASSEMBLY FREE. A comes from abytes.bin written by the same
authoritative unpacker that writes y_ref (dump_gemv_case.py --with-abytes), NOT from a second C++
unpacker. That isolates "does the matrix math scale flat with rows?" from the expensive engineering
question of whether a real kernel can assemble A in registers without paying more than it saves
(llm-scaler's own warning: "assembling it with strided register moves cost more than the DPAS
itself saved"). The cost of this choice: A is 4 bytes per weight vs the trellis's K/8 = 0.375, i.e.
10.7x the bytes at K=3, so GB/s is NOT comparable with rev C and every DPAS number below carries a
byte-traffic handicap that a shipping kernel would not have.

## Correctness

    DPAS bit-exact vs y_ref: True   max|d| 0.0        (E=10, gate_proj, rows=1, KS=4)

Gate A survives untouched - no float anywhere in the path. Getting there caught one bug worth
recording: declaring the B operand `uint8_t` compiles, runs at full speed, and turns every negative
activation into 128..255, inflating the accumulator to ~1.6e8 (the all-same-sign bound) instead of
~5e5. dpas.hpp allows A and B to have different signedness precisely so this mix is expressible.
Note that b70-bench's all-ones self-check is structurally BLIND to this class of bug.

weights = 16.384 M; each cell is min of 3 interleaved runs, burst-of-8 throughput mode.

## What this decides

1. Integer DPAS with byte replication is bit-exact and its rows cost is ~0. The 1.15-slots-per-row
   term in rev C is real and the matrix path removes it.
2. Materializing A in DRAM is NOT viable at decode footprints: at E=10 the 62.5 MiB stream costs
   ~145 us on its own (~360-390 GB/s), which is why rev D loses below rows 6 there. A shipping kernel
   MUST assemble A from trellis bytes in registers/SLM. This probe deliberately did not test that.
3. Therefore step 2 is worth building, with a specific prize: at E=10/rows=4 the ceiling implied by
   combining rev D's flat curve with the trellis's own 5.86 MiB read is roughly 20-30 us against rev
   C's 112.5 us. The whole of that prize rests on assembly costing less than ~2 slots per weight,
   which is the number step 2 must report first, before any rate.

## Is rev D actually byte-bound? (marginal cost, not assertion)

Saying "145 us of A streaming" is an interpretation; the test is to grow A and watch the slope. rev D at
rows=4, KS=4, A = 6.25 MiB per expert:

    E     A MiB       us    us/MiB   implied GB/s
    1      6.25     25.7      4.11          255.0
    2     12.50     34.7      2.78          377.7
    4     25.00     55.1      2.20          475.8
    5     31.25     81.5      2.61          402.1
   10     62.50    147.5      2.36          444.3

Marginal from E=1 to E=10: (147.5 - 25.7) us over 56.25 MiB = **2.17 us/MiB = 484 GB/s**, about 72% of
the measured 674 GB/s copy peak - so the incremental cost is genuinely A streaming, and the intercept is
about 12 us of row-independent, byte-independent fixed cost (the matrix path itself, partial writes,
setup). At E=1 the fixed part dominates and effective GB/s is pessimistic, which is exactly why the
small-E rows curves are flat.

What that implies for step 2, stated as arithmetic rather than hope: replacing A's 4 B/weight with the
trellis's 0.375 B/weight removes ~135 us of the ~147 us at E=10/rows=4, leaving the ~12 us fixed cost
plus whatever the unpack-and-assemble loop adds. So the ceiling for a real DPAS kernel at that shape is
tens of microseconds against rev C's 112.5 us, and the *only* question is whether assembling A costs more
than it saves - which is the number step 2 must print first.

## Cross-check: three independent implementations agree bit for bit

Second configuration, different split and row count (E=2, KS=2, rows=4, A = 12.5 MiB):

    revD(dpas, KS=2, rows=4) bit-exact vs y_ref: True
    revB still bit-exact on the same case:       True
    revD == revB:                                True

That is the NumPy reference, rev B's scalar dp4a loop, and rev D's XMX integer matrix path producing
identical float32 output from identical checkpoint bytes. It also exercises a different KS, so the
partial-sum tree differs by construction and the integer accumulator is confirmed order-independent
across the two paths - which is what makes the DPAS variant substitutable inside the engine's
completion protocol rather than a second numerics regime to validate separately.

## Step 2 (rev E, probe_dpas2.cpp): A assembled from trellis bytes, no byte expansion

Same case, same epilogue, but the kernel now reads ONLY the trellis bytes (5.86 MiB) and builds the A
operand itself: 16 lanes unpack a tile with rev C's validated window math, store each window's four
product bytes as one u32, then four load/mad pairs consume the tile (2 column-blocks x 2 byte-blocks).
B is the activation replicated 4x along K (4k x 16 tokens = 163 KB, L2-resident), staged once per
k-slice and reused across every expert so it is not re-read per expert.

The sourcing of the two operands turned out to matter more than anything else - a 2x2 factorial at
rows=4, KS=8:

    A source        B source       us    slots/weight
    SLM             SLM          372.1        32.5
    SLM             global       191.5        16.7
    global scratch  SLM          456.5        39.9
    global scratch  global       109.1         9.5   <- and bit-exact vs y_ref

Feeding joint_matrix_load from LOCAL (SLM) space costs about 20 slots per weight; the same operand
sourced from global space costs about 2. That is a 3.4x difference on the whole kernel, and it is a
property of the lowering, not of the algorithm: the intuitive design (stage the assembled tile in SLM,
which is fast memory and right next to the math unit) is the slow one here. A 1 KB global scratch region
per sub-group, rewritten every tile, stays in L2 and costs no DRAM traffic at all - it reports 56 GB/s
against the trellis, far below the 292 GB/s the GEMV achieves.

rev E (global scratch A, global B) against rev C, min of 4 interleaved runs, E=10 gate_proj:

    KS   rows |  revC us |  revE us  slots/w | revE vs revC
     8     1 |     75.6 |    153.3     13.4 | x0.49
     8     4 |    112.5 |    109.5      9.6 | x1.03
     8     8 |    252.0 |    113.3      9.9 | x2.22
     8    16 |    488.3 |    122.2     10.7 | x4.00
    16     1 |     75.3 |     95.5      8.3 | x0.79
    16     4 |    112.6 |     99.8      8.7 | x1.13
    16     8 |    251.9 |    108.8      9.5 | x2.32
    16    16 |    484.7 |    127.2     11.1 | x3.81

  best per rows:  rows 1 x0.79 | rows 4 x1.13 | rows 8 x2.31 | rows 16 x3.98

WHAT THIS SETTLES
* The row term is genuinely removable: rev E spends 8.3 - 11.1 slots/weight across rows 1..16 (flat),
  where rev C spends 7.0 at rows=1 and 31 at rows=16. Predicted crossover from the fitted model was
  rows ~2.4; measured is ~3.5, so the model slightly flattered DPAS.
* Assembly is NOT free and this is the number step 2 existed to produce: ~8.5 slots/weight all-in for
  rev E versus ~5.8 for rev C's unpack-only fixed cost, i.e. assembling A and driving the matrix unit
  costs roughly 2.7 slots/weight more than the dp4a path's own fixed work. Because it is a FIXED cost,
  it is repaid once per weight instead of once per token - which is the entire argument for the matrix
  path, and it pays back from rows ~4.
* Consequence for the engine: two decode schedules keyed on rows - dp4a (rev C) at rows <= 2, DPAS
  (rev E) at rows >= 4. Same integer accumulator, so both are bit-exact against the same reference and
  they agree with each other bit-for-bit; the split is a performance decision, not a numerics regime.
* Caveats, stated plainly: rev E is a first cut and unoptimized (matrix objects re-created inside the
  inner loop, two barriers per tile, no double-buffering, scratch geometry untuned), so the x8/x16
  numbers should be read as "a working DPAS path already wins this much" rather than as the ceiling.
  Measured on ONE geometry (E=10, gate_proj, k=2560, n=640, K=3) - the other eight checkpoints,
  including GLM's n=4096/tn=256 and Qwen3.5/3.6's tn=32, are unmeasured, and rows=1 losing means the
  engine carries two code paths that both need Gate A in CI.

## Was "prepare the operands once at load time" measured? Yes - it is rev D, and it loses

rev D *is* the prepare-once design: A pre-expanded in DRAM so the kernel's operand-assembly cost is
literally zero. Swept to its best split-K (min of 3, burst mode) against rev E's best and rev C:

    rows | revD best (assembly FREE, bytes 10.7x) | revE best (assembly on the fly) | rev C
       1 |                     135.8 us (ks=1)    |      95.5 us                    |  76.8 us
       4 |                     137.2 us (ks=1)    |      99.8 us                    | 112.5 us
       8 |                     138.5 us (ks=1)    |     108.8 us                    | 251.8 us
      16 |                     141.8 us (ks=1)    |     122.2 us                    | 483.7 us

rev E beats the free-assembly version at every row count (x1.16 to x1.42), because rev D is entirely
byte-bound: 62.5 MiB of A at rev D's own measured 484 GB/s marginal is 135.4 us, versus 135.8 us
degistered at rows=1 - the math is completely hidden under the streaming. Even at the 674 GB/s copy
peak the floor is 97.2 us, which still loses to rev E everywhere and to rev C at rows=1.

Note also what rev E already is: the unpack runs ONCE per weight per launch regardless of rows - that is
precisely why its curve is flat. So preparing at load time buys no extra amortization; it removes only
the ~5.8-slot unpack and charges 10.7x bytes for it. Capacity makes that unaffordable: 4 B/weight turns
Qwen3.8@2.68 from 36.33 GiB into ~388 GiB against 32,656 MiB of VRAM, collapsing the resident expert
share from h ~ 0.98 to ~ 0.08 and inverting the whole placement plan; a decoded *cache* is no better,
since L2 is 24.0 MiB (measured) and holds ~3.8 experts of expanded bytes. Cheaper prepared forms exist
(10-bit decoded values need >= 1.25 B/weight, e.g. a u4+u8 plane split at 1.5 B/weight with two mads,
still exact) but 3.3-4x bytes still cuts h to ~0.25.

The idea that survives is not expansion but a **same-size repack**: the trellis packs 8 windows into
3K bits, so a lane's windows straddle dword boundaries and cost 3 strided loads plus shifts. Reordering
the ring within a tile - still K bits per weight, zero extra bytes, zero extra DRAM - so one wide load
covers a lane's windows attacks the ~5.8 fixed slots instead of the ~2.7 assembly slots. If the fixed
cost fell to ~3, rev E would cost ~5.5 slots/weight and beat rev C at rows=1 too, collapsing the engine
to one decode schedule. Price: the B70's bytes stop being interchangeable with the 3090/CPU trellis
kernels, plus a load-time pass - the E4c trade-off, so it must clear that bar. Gate E28.

## Would compile-time K help rev E too? Priced, and the answer is "a little"

Same knobs, rev E at its best split-K (KS=16, A in L2 scratch, B global), min of 3:

  rows | full | unpack+assemble only | matrix path only | full - matrix-only | predicted with x1.90 extraction
     1 | 95.5 |                69.3  |        41.5      |   26.2 us (27%)      |  83.1 us  (x1.15)
     4 | 99.7 |                74.7  |        57.0      |   25.0 us (25%)      |  87.9 us  (x1.13)
     8 |127.2 |                83.2  |        55.8      |   44.0 us (35%)      | 106.4 us  (x1.20)
    16 |127.8 |               101.5  |        73.3      |   26.3 us (21%)      | 115.3 us  (x1.11)

Two caveats stated plainly. (1) The halves do not sum (69.3 + 41.5 > 95.5): each knob removes work but
leaves the loop, barrier and store scaffolding in place, so the difference bounds the extraction share
rather than measuring it exactly. (2) Applying rev C's measured x1.90 to that whole share overstates the
win, because K-constancy folds shifts and the straddle select only - it does nothing for the u32 stores
into the A scratch, the two barriers per tile, the matrix loads or the PART stores, which is where most
of rev E's overhead lives.

And rev E has a prerequisite rev C does not. Its lane mapping is `t = lane + 16*tt`, so the intra-dword
offset is `(K*(8*lane + 1) - 16) & 31` - **lane-dependent** (it is invariant only when K*8*lane is a
multiple of 32 for every lane, i.e. K=4). rev C got the win for free because its mapping is
`t = 4*q0 + a4`, where the `K*32*q0` term vanishes mod 32. So rev E needs a lane remap first: keep `a4`
as the unrolled inner index and split `j` across lanes, folding the j-half offset into a per-lane bit
base (one variable shift to form the 64-bit base per tile, then compile-time shifts for the windows).

Verdict: worth doing only as a by-product - the engine will template K for rev C anyway, and sharing one
extractor between the dp4a and DPAS paths removes a second copy of the trickiest inner loop. On its own
merits, x1.1-x1.2 does not justify the remap; rev E's bigger levers are the per-tile barrier pair and the
A scratch round trip itself, which only ESIMD register fragments (xmx::dpas on simd operands) can remove -
this toolchain has no joint_matrix_construct, so the API forces the memory round trip.

## REFUTATION: compile-time K does NOT transfer to rev E, and the aligned lane map is 2.3x WORSE

The prediction above (x1.1-1.2) was wrong, and the change was reverted. Attribution, KS=8, A in L2
scratch, B global, min of 4 interleaved runs, every variant bit-exact against y_ref:

    variant (all same algorithm, same grid)            rows=1  rows=4  rows=8  rows=16
    runtime K, current lane map  (committed rev E)      106.0   109.4   112.9   123.9
    compile-time K only, same lane map                  106.6   109.5   148.1*  122.4    x0.99 - x1.01
    pair-map (aligned groups + constant shifts)         252.7   255.3   259.8   268.1    x0.42 - x0.45
    pair-map, unroll none / a4 / j / both               within 0.5% of each other         no effect

    * single outlier at rows=8; the other three points are within 0.6% of baseline.

So: making K a compile-time constant, which bought rev C x1.90, buys rev E **nothing**. And the lane map
that makes the shifts constant - rev C's own mapping, which is what enabled the win there - makes rev E
2.3x SLOWER. Unrolling was a non-factor: all four pragma combinations produce the same binary in
practice, because the loops have fixed trip counts and are already unrolled (the earlier 2x regression
from runtime loop *bounds* was about the bounds, not the pragma - recorded separately).

WHY, as a hypothesis rather than a measurement: rev C's bottleneck IS the extraction ALU, so folding
shifts and the straddle select removes most of its fixed cost. rev E's bottleneck is the store pattern
into the A scratch. Its current map is accidentally excellent for stores: with t = lane + 16*tt, four
lanes share one column and write adjacent 4-byte slots, i.e. a contiguous 16-byte run per group of four
lanes. rev C's map gives each of 8 lanes a different column, 64 bytes apart, so the same 32 stores per
tile become 8 separate transactions instead of 2. That is worth far more than the arithmetic saved.

WHAT CHANGES:
* The x1.1-1.2 estimate above is dead; do not template K for rev E's sake. (For rev C it stands at x1.90.)
* rev E's real lever is now identified by elimination: the store pattern. Arrange the 32 lanes' u32
  writes to be contiguous in the A region - which is a free choice, since SLM decouples producer lanes
  from fragment mapping - and re-measure. Worth up to the ~25-35% that extraction+store accounts for.
* Unroll pragmas are not a tuning knob on this toolchain for fixed-trip loops; stop budgeting for them.

## Second attempt: dense stores - x0.64, but the experiment was confounded, so the hypothesis survives

Made the 16 lanes of a sub-group write one column's 16 rows per iteration (16 consecutive u32 = 64
contiguous bytes per store instruction, instead of 4 lanes striding every other slot). Bit-exact, and
KS=8, min of 4, A sourcing as noted:

    mapping                 rows=1  rows=4  rows=8  rows=16
    committed (sparse)       106.0   108.8   112.7   122.2
    dense                    165.3   168.9   175.9   181.6     x0.64 at every row count
    (identical result with A in SLM: 106.2 -> 165.4)

But the two mappings do not differ only in store density. Moving the loop to one window per iteration
moved the amortization boundary with it:

    committed (t = lane + 16*tt)   6 dword loads for 16 windows  = 0.375 loads/window
    pair-map (a4 outer)           12 dword loads for 32 windows  = 0.375 loads/window
    dense (c outer, 1 window)     48 dword loads for 16 windows  = 3.000 loads/window

So x0.64 is fully explained by an 8x increase in load instructions, and **store density was never actually
tested** - my "rev E is store-bound" hypothesis is unproven, not refuted. Worth recording because the
confounder was invisible in the code diff: the loads looked identical per iteration, and only the iteration
count changed.

Then the arithmetic says the lever is largely closed anyway. Store density per instruction is capped by the
format's own geometry: an instruction is one j-index across 16 lanes, and (col, r) = (q0 + 8*(j>>2),
2*a4 + (j&1) + 8*(j&2)). Packing 16 consecutive u32 (one column, r = 0..15) requires lanes to vary a4 and
the low bits of j - which is exactly what breaks sharing a V01/V12 dword pair, pushing loads to 0.75 per
window. The committed map sits at the corner of that trade: 0.375 loads/window and 8-byte store runs, and
the alternatives tried cost 2.3x and 1.6x. Two cheap levers dead, both bit-exact, both reverted.

What is left for rev E is the lever neither experiment touched: the scratch round trip itself. Every
mapping keeps `unpack -> store 1 KB -> joint_matrix_load` because this toolchain has no
joint_matrix_construct, so operands must come from memory. Only ESIMD xmx::dpas on register simd operands
removes it - a real rewrite, and the two failures above lower its expected value rather than raising it.

Recommendation, stated as a recommendation: stop optimizing rev E. It already beats rev C by x2.1 at
rows=8 and x3.7 at rows=16, and the remaining effort is better spent on the decisions that are actually
blocked - down_proj and one GLM geometry on rev E (the two-schedule policy currently rests on gate_proj
alone), and the per-K instantiations for rev C's other recipes.

## Item 2 (unconditional SLM allocation) is real, and its effect is zero

Confirmed in code: rev E computes slmTotal = slmB*2 + 2*16*TILE_BYTES with slmB = per_sg*TILE_BYTES*NTOK
derived only from geometry - at KS=8 that is ~544 KiB requested on a device reporting 128 KiB of SLM, and
the launch succeeds anyway (open question, recorded below). Rev F sizes it from the source flags and

Three readings.

1. **The marginal price has two regimes, and the break is L2 capacity (24 MiB).** While weights + partials
   fit, extra PART writes are nearly free - 0.2-0.8 us/MiB, i.e. 1.3-5 TB/s absorbed by L2. Once they do
   not, the price jumps to 1.3-2.2 us/MiB = 450-770 GB/s, which is the DRAM stream rate this card measured
   all project (copy peak 674 GB/s). So PART is a cliff, not a slope, and the cliff is at 128*KSIk
   B/weight where weights + partials + activations cross ~24 MiB - which is why down_proj (k=640, so the
   same KS buys 4x more bytes per weight) pays 3x what gate does at KS=4.
2. **Item 5 is worth roughly 5-11% on gate and 15-25% on down_proj, not the headline it looked like.**
   Single-copy cost is about half a duplicated delta: gate KS=8 ~2.4 us of 88 written, plus a similar
   amount read back by combine; down_proj KS=4 ~8 us of 82 written plus ~8 read. Eliminating PART and the
   combine kernel entirely (structural variant) therefore recovers ~5-10 us on gate and ~16 us on
   down_proj, plus one enqueue that burst timing hides but real decode pays.
3. **PART traffic explains the KS optima, which is a policy result.** Gate gets worse beyond KS=8 (+27.7 us
   duplicated at KS=16 versus +4.8 at KS=8) and down_proj beyond KS=4, exactly where the measured best KS
   sits. E27's launch-policy table should cap KS by a bytes-per-weight bound (keep 128*KSIk <~ 0.4 B/w,
   i.e. KS <~ 0.003*k) rather than only by grid-fill, otherwise the parallelism bought past that point is
   paid back in partials.

## The bigger inefficiency this surfaced, which is not item 5

Chasing PART made the rows=1 loss legible: the DPAS path always computes NTOK=16 token columns, so at
b=1 it spends fifteen sixteenths of its matrix work on lanes that are thrown away. That - not partials -
is why rev F still loses x2.5 to rev C+KCONST at rows=1 while winning x2.4-x4.2 at rows 8-16, and it is
why its rows curve is "flat" in the first place. Specializing the N dimension (8 columns instead of 16 for
rows <= 8) would halve BOTH the mad work and PART traffic at once.

Recorded as not-yet-implemented, deliberately: N is entangled with the B tensor's pitch throughout rev F
(matrix shape vs 16-token pitch, staging stride, and the SLM bulk-copy offsets all use the same NTOK
constant), and splitting those two meanings correctly is a delicate change across ~8 sites where an
off-by-4 would still run fast and produce wrong numbers. Given how this session went, that is worth doing
with a clean bit-exactness check in front of it, not squeezed in behind a price measurement.

================================================================================
Variant (a), examined before building: shrinking the token dimension saves no instructions
================================================================================
The plan was to specialize the accumulator's N (token columns, NTOK=16) down to 8 for rows <= 8, on the
theory that at b=1 fifteen sixteenths of the matrix work is discarded. Working out which loops actually
generate the instruction count kills that theory:

* mad instructions per tile = (column blocks = 2) x (k byte-blocks = MK/KP = 4) = 8, and NEITHER count
  depends on N. Cutting N from 16 to 8 keeps all 8 mads and gives each one half the useful output, while
  int8 DPAS at N=8 almost certainly executes at half the per-instruction throughput. Same instructions,
  less work done.
* What N=8 would save is traffic that 'x' has already taken away: PART halves again, from 0.78 to 0.39 MiB
  on gate_proj - about 0.4 us at the measured 0.2-0.8 us/MiB in-L2 rate. On down_proj 3.12 -> 1.56 MiB,
  maybe 1.3 us. Against a 73.7 us kernel that is noise, and it costs the delicate N-versus-pitch split
  across ~12 sites where an off-by-4 runs fast and is wrong.
* The x1.85 loss at rows=1 is therefore not token-lane waste. Slot arithmetic: psux at rows=1 costs
  73.7 us for 16.384 M weights = 6.4 slots/weight, against rev C+KCONST's 3.5. The DPAS path is simply a
  more expensive kernel per weight when there is one token to amortize it over - which is the same
  conclusion the original crossover gave, now with the traffic excuse removed.

The instruction-count lever that does exist is the other dimension. Each A tile already holds 16 output
columns (r = 0..15), but they are consumed as two MM=8 accumulators, c0 and c1, so every tile issues two
mads per byte-block where one M=16 mad would cover the same output. Consolidating to a single MM=16 x N=16
accumulator per tile would:

  - halve the mads (8 -> 4 per tile per byte-block loop, i.e. half the mad stream at every row count),
  - halve the PART stores (two 8x16 stores -> one 16x16), and
  - remove one of the two joint_matrix_load(A) calls per byte-block.

Unlike the N change this pays at rows=1 too, which is where the DPAS path is losing, and it needs no new
layout: the A tile is already assembled column-major with r as the column index. The open question is
whether the toolchain accepts joint_matrix<..., accumulator, 16, 16> with the int8 operand shapes this
kernel uses (bench.cpp's reference uses M=8, N=16, K=32 for int8), and whether the r -> (row, column-block)
map in the store survives consolidation. Both are cheap to test: it is one new flag, and bit-exactness is
against the same y_ref that has caught every other mistake in this line of work.

Recorded as analysis, explicitly not measurement: no N=8 build was made and no M=16 build exists yet. The
difference between those two statements is the whole point of writing this section.

================================================================================
Can the DPAS path be built for exactly one row, known at compile time?  No - and the
reason turns out to point at a bigger lever than the one the question was about.
================================================================================
Made the token width a build knob (-DNTOK_OVERRIDE=8|4; every consumer of NTOK is consistent, so this
narrows the real operand, it is not a mask) and built rev F at each width:

    N=16  builds, runs, bit-exact at rows 1/4/8
    N=8   BUILDS FINE, then throws at launch, for every flag combination (none, p, x, psux, su, u):
            "joint_matrix with parameters matrix_type::sint32, use::accumulator, Rows=8, Cols=8 is not
             supported on this device"
    N=4   same rejection (Rows=8, Cols=4); rows=8 additionally refused by the R <= NTOK guard

So the smallest accumulator this toolchain will lower on B70 is 8x16 - the rejection is about the
accumulator's *area*, not about row count, and it is a runtime device-support check with no
static_assert and no readable table in the headers. A compile-time single-row DPAS variant is therefore not
expressible at all: the question "could the compiler optimize it" never gets asked, because the operand
cannot be built. And the earlier instruction count says the question was not worth much anyway: mads per
tile are column blocks x k byte-blocks, independent of N, so a hypothetical N=1 DPAS would issue the same 8
mads per tile and save only the PART that 'x' already cut (~0.4 us of 73.7).

## The single-row variant does exist. We already have it, and it is faster.

Collapse the token dimension of the same algebra to one column and the DPAS step becomes

    C[m] = sum_k A[m,k] * x_k        with A[m, 4j..4j+3] = the four product bytes of weight j

which is exactly rev C's `dot_acc(prod, splat(x))`: four weight-byte x activation products per instruction,
none of the matrix assembly, no operand stores, no 16-deep partials. Measured at rows=1: rev C+KCONST 3.5
slots/weight (39.7 us) against the DPAS path's 6.4 (73.7 us). So "a DPAS variant that only does one row" is
the dp4a kernel by another name, and the two-schedule policy keyed on rows *is* the compile-time row-count
specialization - the compiler gets to know the row count because it picks a different kernel per range.

## Where the shape question does pay: K per mad, not N

One row of the (invalid, see below) shape probe agrees with independent evidence and is worth chasing:
M=8, N=16, K=32 runs and computes correctly, and that is precisely the vendor bench's int8 shape
(`run_matrix<int8_t, int32_t, 8, 16, 32>`, measured at 366.92 of 367 TOPS). rev F uses K=8 per mad (MK=8:
two weights per mad, since each weight contributes 4 product bytes). Because

    mads per tile = (output columns / M) x (K bytes / K per mad)

widening K from 8 to 32 would cut the mad stream 4x - four times the 2x available from the M=16
consolidation in 13n, and unlike the N question it pays at rows=1, where this path loses. The A scratch
already stores 16 weights x 4 bytes per column, so 8 weights per mad fits inside one tile row with no new
layout. Stated as a hypothesis, not a result: legality must be established by changing rev F itself (the
trusted construction), not by the standalone probe, and the test is the same y_ref comparison that has
caught everything else here.

## The throwaway shape probe was invalid, so it was deleted rather than annotated

A standalone enumerator was written to map the legal `joint_matrix` shapes. It reported that M=8, N=16,
K=8 is unsupported - the shape rev F runs continuously - and on that basis rejected every M=16 accumulator
and most of the N sweep. Two causes found, one never fixed: `joint_matrix_fill` has its own shape
restrictions (switching to `joint_matrix_load` from global changed nothing), and the residual difference
from rev F's construction was never isolated; work-group shape (16 threads / 1 sub-group vs 32 / 2) made no
difference either. It initially stayed in the tree with a KNOWN-INVALID banner at the top of the file, and
that was the wrong call: a committed instrument that contradicts a working kernel is more likely to be
misread by someone later than to remind them of anything, so it has been removed. Nothing here depends on
it - the N=8/N=4 results come from rev F itself, which is its own oracle, and the K=32 shape that motivated
the K question is corroborated independently by `doc/b70/b70-bench/bench.cpp`'s int8 configuration
(`run_matrix<int8_t, int32_t, 8, 16, 32>`, measured at 366.92 of 367 TOPS), not by the probe. Lesson kept:
LESSONS 40, calibrate an oracle on a case you already know passes before believing one negative from it.

================================================================================
K per mad is already 32. The real cost split says the target is assembly, not mads.
================================================================================
The K-widening idea came from reading MK=8 somewhere and concluding rev F issued K=8 mads. It does not:
`static constexpr int MK = 32; // matrix K for int8 = 8 weights x 4 bytes`, two kb blocks per trellis tile,
so each tile issues 2 (kb) x 2 (column blocks) = 4 mads covering 16 weights - **0.25 mads per weight**, at
the same 8x16x32 int8 shape the vendor bench measures at 366.92 of 367 TOPS. There is no wider K to move
to, and the mad stream was never the cost.

That in turn discredits a number I had been quoting from this file: "the mad path costs +62.7 us". The
DPAS_NO_MAD ablation skips the whole tile-math call, *operand loads included*, so it priced loads and mads

joint_matrix_load, drops only joint_matrix_mad) and DPAS_NO_STORE (drops the PART store) - and ran the

## What this retracts

* "Widen K per mad to quarter the mad stream" - void, K is already 32. The invalid shape probe left that
  residue in my reasoning; deleting the file did not delete the idea it seeded, which is worth knowing about
  how bad instruments do their damage.
* Item 13n's recommendation to consolidate the two MM=8 accumulators into one 16x16, "halving the mad
  stream", is **not worth building**: the mad stream is 0.13 slots/weight (2 %), so halving it buys ~1 %,
  and the other things consolidation would remove are half the A loads (~0.7 slots/weight, ~10 %) and the
  PART stores, which just measured free. Correct arithmetic on the wrong cost model still produced a
  plausible-sounding plan; only the measurement killed it.
* The earlier claim that operand sourcing (SLM vs global) was a first-order effect survives, but note it is
  the *load* term it acts on, and that term is 21 % - not the 4x kernel-wide swing rev E showed, which the
  packed-B and barrier work has since removed.

## What it points at

The DPAS kernel is 66 % trellis unpack plus assembly into the A operand layout, and another 21 % moving
those assembled bytes into matrix registers. Both are consequences of the same thing: this toolchain has no
joint_matrix_construct, so A must be written to memory and re-read. That is the ESIMD register-operand route
(`xmx::dpas` on `simd` operands) - previously the "last resort", now the only remaining lever, and its
ceiling is most of the kernel: fixed + mads is ~9.4 us of 74.3. It also matters *because* rows 4-16 is
where the DPAS path is chosen: assembly cost is batch-independent, so cutting it moves the regime the
policy actually uses, and would drag the crossover toward rows=1 as a side effect.

Feasibility first, and calibrated this time: `doc/b70/b70-bench/bench.cpp` already contains a working
`run_matrix<int8_t, int32_t, 8, 16, 32>` ESIMD kernel, so the shapes and the call exist and are known-good -
a probe of this route starts from a configuration that is measured to run at full rate, not from a shape
guess.

================================================================================
REV G (probe_dpas4.cpp, tokens on M): runs on the B70 for the first time, bit-exact, and
it changes the rows schedule. Outside authorship; measured here.
================================================================================
Rev G swaps the operands: A = activation (int8, replicated 4x along K, M = token rows), B = weights
(uint8, ext_intel_packed, 32 bytes x 16 output columns), so C is [token][column] and the token dimension
moves onto M, which the hardware lets us shrink, instead of N, which XMX fixes at 16 for int8. Assembly is
unchanged in substance - a packed B dword is one weight's four product bytes - at a transposed address, and
partials keep only the padded token rows. He had validated 384 combinations under the host emulator and
never run it on this card.

Correctness on the B70, against each case's own y_ref: bit-exact for gate_proj at K = 2, 3 and 5, rows 1/4/8
and 16, KS in {4, 8, 16}, and for down_proj at KS in {2, 4, 10}. Independence check: at rows=1 rev G's output
is bit-identical to rev C+KCONST running the same bytes through a completely different instruction path.

shape of bug that a fresh-file guard turns into an error instead of a verdict. Recorded rather than

## What actually produced the win, since it is not the advertised mechanism

His model predicts DPAS work scales with M, so matrix cost should fall ~8x going M=8 -> M=1. Forced at
rows=1, KS=8 (rev G, min of 3):

    M                1      2      4      8
    full            56.3   58.0   58.8   77.1 us
    matrix only     24.4   25.3   25.7   29.1 us     ratio M=8/M=1 = 1.19, not 8

The matrix term barely moves, which is what this file's factorial predicted from the other direction: mads
are 0.13 slots/weight, 2 % of the kernel, so no reparametrisation of the matrix shape can be the headline.
M still buys x1.37 on the full kernel at rows=1 - through the A operand loads (M rows of activation read per
byte-block) and the partial rows that no longer need padding - and the remaining gain comes from the
third thing he changed: because partials now scale with rows, KS can go to 16 (grid 640 groups instead of
400) without paying the partial traffic that made high KS self-defeating in rev F. Attribution matters here
because the same reasoning says the next win is structural, not more shape tuning: rev G still loops
experts inside the thread, so porting the 'x' grid (experts in the grid, KS -> 1) onto rev G's layout should
remove the KS sensitivity that currently makes it lose x1.50 to rev F at rows=16 on down_proj, where its
best KS collapses from 16 to 2.

## joint_matrix_apply is refuted as the in-register route

DPAS_REG=1 (build B in registers with joint_matrix_apply, no SLM at all) is **bit-exact** - his
fill-before-apply fix holds, and I verified exactness before timing rather than after - but it is the wrong
direction: 96.3 us at rows=1 against 41.5 with SLM, and 452.8 us at rows=8 against 53.3, i.e. x2.3 and x8.5
slower, degrading with M. That is the answer to the question he flagged as unknown: the per-element
coordinate loop does not lower to register moves, it lowers to per-element work. If B is ever to be fed from
registers it has to be ESIMD `xmx::dpas`, which is the rewrite, not this one-liner.

What this settles.
* **The "no spills" claim reproduces on our driver** (0 vs 11 references), as does the mechanism behind rev

================================================================================
LOW-LEVEL LOOK AT REV H: it is unpack-bound, NOT byte-bound, and not prefetch-bound
================================================================================
Three experiments, all warm + interleaved, rev H + DPAS_X at its own optimum (ks2), control runs matching
the committed binary to 0.1 us so the builds were comparable.

1. **Ablation ladder - the matrix path is nearly free, the unpack is everything.**

     gate rows=1:  full 39.1   unpack-only 38.3   matrix-only 10.7  -> unpack 28.4 us, matrix 0.8 us
     gate rows=16: full 44.2   unpack-only 42.9   matrix-only 16.3  -> unpack 27.9 us, matrix 1.3 us
     down rows=1:  full 43.2   unpack-only 41.4   matrix-only 11.7  -> unpack 31.5 us, matrix 1.8 us
     down rows=16: full 64.4   unpack-only 62.7   matrix-only 29.8  -> unpack 34.6 us, matrix 1.7 us

   Consistent with the ISA census (~104 instructions per tile, 2 `dpas.8x1`, 0 SLM, 20 syncs): XMX costs
   about a microsecond and a half. Everything worth attacking is in the unpack.

2. **Prefetch depth does nothing.** PF_DIST exposed as -DPF_DIST_OVERRIDE; tiles fetched ahead 2/3/4 at
   gate rows 1/4/8/16: 39.1/39.7/41.2/44.2, 39.1/39.4/42.5/44.8, 39.4/40.4/41.8/44.1 - all within noise, and
   PF=5 (built without the per-kernel code split, so its burst absorbed JIT) was uniformly worse. So the
   trellis load latency is already hidden, and the "deeper prefetch" idea from the original review is
   measured-dead for this kernel.

3. **It is not byte-bound either - and this refutes what I wrote in item 13r.** Same 16.384M-weight problem,
   trellis K varied (rev H instantiates K=2..5, so the same kernel, same weight count, different bytes):

     K    MiB   revH rows=1   revH rows=16   effective GB/s
     2   3.91        39.1          43.0           105
     3   5.86        39.1          44.2           157
     5   9.77        39.3          43.7           261
     ratios 1.00 / 1.00 / 1.01   where byte-proportional predicts 1.00 / 1.50 / 2.50

   A 2.5x byte range changes the time by 1 %. The dp4a path is flat in K the same way (76.3 / 75.5 / 76.5 us
   runtime-K), which is the recipe-independence result from item 2b still holding. So my sentence in 13r -
   "at ~0.32 instructions/weight the DRAM floor is close enough that bytes and locality will pay more than
   further instruction cuts" - was wrong, and it was the *prediction* that justified stopping instruction
   work. Corrected below.

What the three together leave: the unpack costs ~1 us per tile (40 tiles/thread, ~2000 cycles) while issuing
only ~104 SIMD instructions for it, it is indifferent to bytes and to load distance. That is a per-tile
serialization, and the ISA shows two candidates I have not yet tested in isolation:
* **the same 96-byte tile is read into all 16 lanes** (`load.ugm.d64x8t.a64` x2 per tile, each lane taking 24
  dwords), i.e. 1536 bytes of LSU/L1 traffic for 96 bytes of data - a 16x amplification of the one resource
  that the K-sweep says we are not short of otherwise;
* **`mov` is rev H's largest opcode class (141)**, which is the products being shuffled into the DPAS B
  operand image plus that lane-duplicated data moving twice.

Next experiment, in order of promise: restructure the tile load so each lane loads a *distinct* slice and the
windows are exchanged by shuffle/select rather than each lane reading the whole tile - which attacks both
candidates at once - and only then revisit the A-operand pipeline (note the matrix-only rung shows the A load
is inside the 10.7 us, so it is a smaller prize than it looked from the ISA alone: `sync.nop {$12.dst}` next
to the dpas is real but bounded by that 10.7 us, not by the 28 us unpack).

Correction to my own record: the flat-in-K result means the decode kernels here are *neither* bandwidth-bound
nor issue-bound; they are bound by per-tile data movement inside the thread. "We are near the DRAM floor"
was a story told from two of the three required measurements (instructions and bytes, without the K sweep).
